Who passes, who scores: the assist network of the champions

01 Background

Box scores tell how many assists a player made and how many points he scored, but not who passed to whom. Two teams with the same number of assists can play in very different ways: in one, a single guard feeds everyone; in another, the ball goes through many hands and ends with the same two players.

This project looks at the relationships between players rather than at their individual totals. It uses the play-by-play log, which records every assist next to the basket it created, to build the network of who assists whom. It then asks three questions about the 2025-26 champions:

  • Around which players does the offence turn? Who mostly creates baskets for others, and who mostly finishes them?
  • Who creates his own shot? Some players score a lot without an assist, and the assist network alone does not show them.
  • Is the champion's passing more spread out or more concentrated than the rest of the league?

The analysis is descriptive: it shows how this team played in one season. It does not claim that this way of playing makes a team win.

02 Data & tools

  • Data source: Euroleague & Eurocup Datasets (Kaggle). Three files: the play-by-play log (every event of every game, in order), the box score (official statistics of every player in every game) and the game headers (teams, phase of the competition, final scores).
  • Tools: Python, pandas, numpy, networkx (network metrics), matplotlib.
  • Scope: the 43 games played by Olympiacos in 2025-26 (regular season, playoffs and Final Four) for the analysis of the team, and the 380 games of the 2025-26 regular season for the comparison with the league.

03 What is network analysis

Network analysis (also called social network analysis when the elements are people) studies systems made of elements and of the connections between them. Instead of describing each element on its own, it describes the structure of the connections: who is connected to whom, how strongly, and which elements hold the structure together. It is used, for example, to study friendships in a school, citations between scientific papers or links between web pages. In basketball, the elements are the players and the connections are passes.

The vocabulary used in the rest of the report:

Node
One element of the network. Here, a node is a player.
Edge (or arc)
A connection between two nodes. Here, an edge from player A to player B means that A made at least one assist to B.
Directed network
A network where every edge has a direction, drawn as an arrow. Direction matters here: "Walkup assists Vezenkov" is not the same as "Vezenkov assists Walkup", and the two are counted separately.
Weighted network
A network where every edge carries a number, its weight. Here, the weight of the edge from A to B is the number of assists A made to B over the season. A thick arrow is a frequent connection, a thin arrow a rare one.
Out-strength and in-strength
The sum of the weights of the edges that leave a node (out-strength) and of those that arrive at it (in-strength). For a player, out-strength is the number of assists he made, in-strength the number of his baskets that were assisted.
Centrality
A family of measures of how important a node is in the network. There is no single definition of importance, so there are several centrality measures. This report uses three of them, explained in section 05: hub score, authority score and PageRank.

Why assists and not passes

The ideal network would contain every pass. The play-by-play, however, records only the assist: the last pass before a made basket, when the pass directly leads to the score. Passes that lead to a missed shot, a turnover or simply another pass are not recorded. So this network does not show how the ball moves around the court. It shows how baskets are created: who delivers the final pass and who converts it.

04 From the play-by-play to the network

In the play-by-play, an assist is a separate event, written next to the basket it created. The assist event names the passer, the basket event names the scorer. To build an edge, every assist has to be matched to its basket.

The matching looks at the events around each assist. Most of the time (84% in the whole dataset) the assist is written right after the basket, sometimes right before. In between there can be events that do not interrupt the action, such as a substitution, a foul or a timeout, and these are skipped. If the nearest event is a made basket of the same team, the two are linked and the edge passer → scorer gains one assist.

Two details:

  • Assists on free throws. Under FIBA statistics rules, an assist can be credited when a pass leads to a shooting foul and the fouled player makes free throws. These assists are kept in the network, because the pass did create points. One assist is one arrow, whatever the number of free throws made.
  • Errors in the log. A few assists cannot be linked to any basket (end of a period, a turnover next to them) and two record the same player as passer and scorer. These are left out.
The matching was checked against the official box score. Of the 920 assists credited to Olympiacos, 894 (97.2%) were linked to a basket: 474 on two-pointers, 308 on three-pointers and 112 on free throws. Over the whole 2025-26 regular season, the share of linked assists is between 94.9% and 98.1% for every team.

Rates and the rotation

A player who is on the court for 1,000 minutes has more chances to pass and to score than one who plays 300. To compare them, counts are turned into rates per 30 minutes: how many assists (or baskets) the player produces in 30 minutes on the court. The choice of 30 is only a scale, close to the minutes of a regular starter; with 40 or 36 every rate would change by the same factor and the ranking of the players would not change.

$$\text{rate per 30 minutes} = \frac{\text{count}}{\text{minutes played}} \times 30$$

A rate computed on very few minutes is unstable: two assists in 15 minutes become 4 per 30 minutes. For this reason the charts and tables show only the rotation, the players who were on the court for at least 10% of the team's game time. For Olympiacos this means at least 174 of the roughly 1,735 minutes of game time, and keeps 13 players. Players below the threshold remain in the network and in the team totals.

05 The Olympiacos assist network

How to read it:

  • Every circle is a player of the rotation. Its size is proportional to the minutes played.
  • Every arrow goes from the passer to the scorer. Its width is proportional to the number of assists between the two. Pairs with fewer than 9 assists over the season (1% of the team's linked assists) are not drawn, otherwise the chart becomes unreadable. This is why Joseph and McKissic, who played fewer minutes, appear without arrows.
  • The colour is the role index, explained in section 06: green for players who mostly give assists, coral for players who mostly receive them.
  • Players are placed on a circle from the most creating, at the top, to the most finishing, at the bottom, alternating left and right. Arrows that go from top to bottom are passes from creators to finishers.

The thickest arrow in the chart goes from Thomas Walkup to Sasha Vezenkov: 92 assists over the season, 10.3% of all the assists of the team. Walkup made 23.0% of the team's assists, more than any other player. Vezenkov received 226 assists, the highest number in the team. Other frequent connections are Dorsey → Vezenkov (30), Walkup → Milutinov (26), Dorsey → Milutinov (25) and Fournier → Hall (25).

Hub and authority scores

Counting assists treats them all the same. The HITS algorithm (Hyperlink-Induced Topic Search), designed to rank web pages, adds a second idea: the quality of the partner. It gives every node two scores, defined in terms of each other:

  • A player has a high hub score if he passes often to players with a high authority score, that is, to the main finishers.
  • A player has a high authority score if he receives often from players with a high hub score, that is, from the main creators.

The two definitions refer to each other, so the scores are computed by repetition: start with equal scores, update the hub scores from the authority scores and the authority scores from the hub scores, weighting every arrow by its number of assists, and repeat until the values stop changing. The scores are then rescaled so that they add up to 1 across all players: each value can be read as a player's share of the team's "creating" (hub) or "finishing" (authority) weight.

A consequence of this definition is that the two scores reinforce each other. Walkup's hub score is high also because his main target is Vezenkov, the player with the highest authority, and the other way round.

PageRank

PageRank is the algorithm originally used by Google to rank web pages. Imagine a ball that travels along the arrows of the network: at each step it moves from a player to one of the players he assists, choosing the arrows in proportion to their number of assists, and every so often (15% of the steps) it jumps to a random player instead. PageRank is the share of time the ball spends on each player. A player has a high PageRank if many assists arrive at him, especially from players who themselves receive many assists. In this network it is mainly a measure of how much a player is a destination of the team's passing. The values add up to 1.

06 Creators and finishers

In this report, a creator is a player who makes more assists than he receives: he mostly sets up baskets for teammates. A finisher is a player who receives more assists than he makes: his baskets mostly come from a teammate's pass. The two labels describe a player's position in the assist network, not his quality or his position on the court.

To place every player on a single scale, the report uses a role index:

$$\text{role index} = \frac{\text{assists given} - \text{assists received}}{\text{assists given} + \text{assists received}}$$

The index goes from -1 (the player never assists, every one of his involvements is receiving) to +1 (he never receives an assist). At 0 he gives as many as he receives. For example, a player with 60 assists given and 20 received has an index of (60 - 20) / (60 + 20) = +0.5.

How to read it: the dashed diagonal is where a player gives as many assists as he receives. Players below the diagonal give more than they receive (creators), players above it receive more than they give (finishers). The farther from the diagonal, the more marked the role.

The players split into two clear groups and none of them sits on the diagonal. The finishers are Vezenkov, Hall, Jones, Peters and Milutinov. The creators are all the others, with Walkup far to the right: almost 7 assists per 30 minutes, about 60% more than Joseph (4.3), the next one.

07 Who creates his own shot

The assist network has a blind spot. A basket scored without an assist does not appear in it, because there is no pass to draw. A player who often scores off the dribble therefore receives few assists and looks like a creator, even if part of his game is creating shots for himself rather than for others. Two further measures fill this gap.

Assisted share

The assisted share is the part of a player's made field goals (two-pointers and three-pointers, free throws excluded) that came from an assist:

$$\text{assisted share} = \frac{\text{assisted two-pointers made} + \text{assisted three-pointers made}}{\text{two-pointers made} + \text{three-pointers made}}$$

A high share means the player scores mostly after a teammate's pass; a low share means he often scores on his own. At team level it is computed on the totals of the team, not as an average of the players' percentages. Olympiacos had 58.4% of made field goals assisted over the 43 games: 52.3% of two-pointers and 71.3% of three-pointers. Three-pointers are assisted more often in every team, because they are usually taken after a pass to an open shooter.

Baskets after an offensive rebound

Not every unassisted basket is a shot created off the dribble. When a player grabs an offensive rebound (a rebound of his own team's missed shot) and scores, there is no assist, but the shot was not created from scratch either. This matters mostly for big men.

The play-by-play does not record what happens between the rebound and the shot: an immediate tip-in, two dribbles and a shot, a pump fake or a spin move all look the same. So the analysis does not try to isolate tip-ins. It identifies a simpler and reliable event: an unassisted basket whose previous action, skipping substitutions, fouls and timeouts, is an offensive rebound of the same team. These are called baskets after an offensive rebound, or second-chance baskets.

In the team, 21.1% of unassisted baskets came right after an offensive rebound, and in almost all of these cases (18.6% of unassisted baskets) the rebounder was the scorer himself.

Self-created baskets

Removing these second-chance baskets gives the measure used in the next chart:

$$\text{self-created per 30 minutes} = \frac{\text{unassisted field goals made} - \text{those right after an offensive rebound}}{\text{minutes played}} \times 30$$

It is the closest available measure of shots a player creates for himself. It is not perfect: it still includes unassisted baskets in transition, for example a player who steals the ball and runs to the basket alone.

08 Olympiacos against the league

The numbers seen so far describe one team. To know whether they are high or low, they have to be compared with the other teams of the same season. The comparison uses only the 2025-26 regular season (38 games for every team), because playoff and Final Four games are played by some teams only, against stronger opponents and often with shorter rotations. For this reason the Olympiacos values in this section differ slightly from those above.

Teams of different seasons are not compared: rosters and coaches change every year, so the same club in two seasons is in practice two different teams.

The effective number

To measure how spread out the passing of a team is, the report uses the effective number of contributors (in economics it is the inverse of the Herfindahl-Hirschman index, a common measure of market concentration). Take each player's share of the team's assists, square it, add the squares, and invert the sum:

$$N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{1}{\sum_i s_i^2}, \qquad s_i = \frac{\text{assists of player } i}{\text{assists of the team}}$$

It answers the question: "how many players, all passing the same amount, would give the same concentration?" Two examples with four players:

  • Each makes 25% of the assists: \(N_{\text{eff}} = 1 / (4 \times 0.25^2) = 4\). The team behaves like four equal passers.
  • One makes 70%, the other three 10% each: \(N_{\text{eff}} = 1 / (0.7^2 + 3 \times 0.1^2) = 1.92\). Four players pass, but the team behaves as if it had fewer than two.

The same formula is applied to assists given (effective number of creators) and to assisted baskets received (effective number of finishers).

Relative to the rotation

The effective number depends also on how many players a coach uses. A team that spreads its minutes over ten players will almost automatically have more creators than a team that plays seven. To remove this effect, the same formula is applied to minutes played, which gives the effective number of players in the rotation, and the effective numbers of creators and finishers are divided by it:

$$\text{creators relative to rotation} = \frac{N_{\text{eff}}(\text{assists given})}{N_{\text{eff}}(\text{minutes played})}$$

and the same for finishers. A value of 1 means that assists are spread among players exactly as playing time is. A value below 1 means they are more concentrated than playing time: fewer players handle the passing (or the finishing) than the players who share the court.

How to read it: teams on the right spread their assists over more passers; teams at the top spread their assisted baskets over more scorers. Team codes are three-letter abbreviations of the club names (for example OLY Olympiacos, ZAL Zalgiris, BAY Bayern Munich, FNR Fenerbahce).

  • Olympiacos is alone in the lower-right area. Its passing is more spread out than the league average: 0.79 creators relative to rotation, fourth of 20 and practically level with Partizan, behind only Bayern (1.02) and Virtus Bologna (0.83). Its finishing is the most concentrated of the league: 0.69 finishers relative to rotation, level with Zalgiris for the lowest value. Many players pass, and their passes converge on a few scorers.
  • The heaviest pair of the league. Walkup → Vezenkov accounts for 9.8% of the regular season assists of Olympiacos, the highest share for a single pair among the 20 teams (Zalgiris 8.9%, Hapoel Tel Aviv 8.7%).
  • No team reaches 1 for finishers. In every team, assisted baskets are more concentrated than playing time. For creators only Bayern goes above 1.

Olympiacos is also the team with the highest share of assisted baskets: 59.2%, 2.7 points above Monaco (56.5%) and 5.5 above the league (53.7%). Almost all the difference comes from two-pointers: 53.0% assisted, the highest in the league, against 45.2% for the league as a whole. On three-pointers Olympiacos (72.6%) is in line with the league (72.4%). A likely reason, consistent with section 07, is that its main finishers are big men and shooters who score mostly near the basket or after a pass.

09 Takeaways

  • Spread-out passing, concentrated finishing. Olympiacos had more creators than the league average and the fewest finishers relative to its rotation. Many players passed, and the passes converged on a small group of scorers.
  • One dominant connection. Walkup → Vezenkov was the most frequent passer-scorer pair of the season, with 92 assists over 43 games and the highest share of any pair in the league.
  • Two different kinds of creator. Walkup set up others and rarely created his own shot. Dorsey and Fournier did both, and were by far the team's main source of self-created baskets, a role the assist network alone does not show.
  • Finishers who did not create their own shots. Every finisher of the rotation was below the team average in self-created baskets; for Hall and Milutinov, most unassisted baskets came right after an offensive rebound.
  • The most assisted offence of the league, with the gap coming from two-pointers.

10 Limitations

  • Assists, not passes. The network contains only the last pass before a made basket. Passes before a miss, a turnover or another pass are invisible, so the analysis describes how baskets were created, not how the ball moved.
  • Assists are a judgement. They are credited by the statisticians at the game, with some subjectivity, and about 3% of them could not be linked to a basket.
  • One team, one season. The results describe how Olympiacos played in 2025-26. They do not show that this structure causes winning: other teams have won with very different profiles.
  • Second chances and transition. The analysis cannot tell a tip-in from a post move after an offensive rebound, and self-created baskets still include unassisted baskets in transition.
  • Small samples. Players with few minutes have unstable rates. They are kept out of the charts with a 10% minutes threshold, which is a choice and not a rule.
